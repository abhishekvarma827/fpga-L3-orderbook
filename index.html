Hardware L3 Order Book on FPGA

A full-depth NASDAQ ITCH order book for 4 stocks in SystemVerilog. It keeps every live order of AAPL, MSFT, NVDA and TSLA in one shared UltraRAM store, meets timing at 250 MHz on the Zynq UltraScale+ K26, and replayed a full real trading day of all four on the KR260 board with every one of 4,084,599 results exact.

Why 4 stocks? On-chip memory, not logic, sets the limit. Every stock needs its own bid and ask price ladders: 28 block RAM tiles per stock. With the board's message and result memories that is 133.5 of the K26's 144 tiles (93%), while logic is only 42% used. A 5th stock would need 28 more tiles. The order store is shared and still has room (42% full at the busiest moment), so the next step is to share the ladders too: one level table in the spare UltraRAM and one ladder engine for all stocks, for 16 to 32 stocks on the same chip.

    1What the design does

    NASDAQ's ITCH feed reports every order event on the exchange: add, cancel, execute, delete and replace. A level-3 book keeps every live order, so after each message it can answer the question a trading system needs: what are the best bid and best ask, and how many shares sit there.

    This design does that for 4 stocks at once. Each message carries a symbol slot (0–3). Adds bring their price and side; reduces (cancel, execute, delete) carry only an order reference, so the book looks the order up in its own store to learn its symbol, side and price. All four stocks share one 196,608-order store; each stock has its own pair of price ladders and its own top-4 levels per side.

    On the board, the ARM processor replays a recorded day through the FPGA in runs of 2,048 messages and checks every result against a Python reference book. The animation at the top follows one message through the whole system.

    Order store u_orders

    Two-choice hashed table, 2 × 8,192 buckets × 12 ways = 196,608 orders, 72-bit entries in 52 UltraRAMs. Shared by all 4 symbols; an entry remembers its order's symbol, side, price level and shares.

    Price ladders g_lad[0..7]

    8 ladders: one per symbol and side. A 3-level bitmap (16 × 64 × 64 one-cent levels) finds the next price level in a few cycles; a level table (4,096 levels) holds each level's total shares.

    Top-4 cache order_book_v3

    The best 4 levels of each side of each symbol are held in registers, so most messages update the top of book without waiting for a ladder search.

    Board wrapper book_board

    AXI-Lite registers, a 2,048-message replay RAM and a result RAM. The ARM writes a block of messages, starts a run, and reads back the top of book of each message's symbol.

    2Block design

    The Zynq UltraScale+ processor drives the book through its M_AXI_HPM0_FPD port. A Clocking Wizard makes the 250 MHz core clock from pl_clk0 (requested 100 MHz; the processor's PLL delivers 96.97 MHz); a SmartConnect bridges to the board wrapper's AXI-Lite port.

    Finding. Block RAM is the limit: 133.5 of 144 tiles. The 8 ladders use 14 tiles each, the same as in the 1-symbol build, so shrinking each level table from 8,192 to 4,096 levels saved nothing. Those tables are wide (8 ways per bucket) rather than deep, and a BRAM tile has a fixed width, so fewer rows do not mean fewer tiles. Shrinking the replay RAM did work (about 86 → 21.5 tiles), which is what made room for 4 symbols. Going beyond 4 symbols needs the level tables moved into a shared UltraRAM table.

    7What limits it, and what comes next

    Memory, not logic. Block RAM is 93% used and logic only 42%. The price ladders take 84% of the block RAM, so 4 symbols is the most this architecture fits on the K26. Moving the ladders' level tables into one shared UltraRAM table and sharing one ladder engine between symbols would allow 16 to 32 symbols.

    Timing margin. The board build meets 250 MHz with 0.002 ns to spare. The worst path runs from an order-store UltraRAM through a 12-bit popcount; most of its delay is wiring. Keeping a fabric register after the UltraRAM and registering the ÷100 multiplier inside its DSP should give 0.3–0.5 ns, enough to try 263–278 MHz on the board.

    End-to-end speed. The FPGA spends 13% of a replay working; the ARM's copies over AXI-Lite take the rest. Feeding messages from 10G Ethernet or DMA instead would let the board run at the FPGA's 14.4 M messages per second.
